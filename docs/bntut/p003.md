Query 2: A marginal probability  
Question. On any given night, what is the probability that the alarm goes off?  
Alarm depends on its two parents, so we sum over all four combinations of B and E. Each term is P(b) ·  
P(e) · P(a \| b, e), and B and E are independent so their probabilities simply multiply.  
  
  
        B            E                   P(B) · P(E)                  P(a \| B, E)       Product  
        true         true          0.001 × 0.002 = 0.000002               0.95          0.0000019  
        true        false          0.001 × 0.998 = 0.000998               0.94          0.0009381  
        false        true          0.999 × 0.002 = 0.001998               0.29          0.0005794  
        false       false          0.999 × 0.998 = 0.997002               0.001         0.0009970  
  
  
        P(a) = 0.0000019 + 0.0009381 + 0.0005794 + 0.0009970 ≈ 0.00252  
Interpretation. The alarm sounds about one night in four hundred. Notice that the false alarm row (no  
burglary, no earthquake) contributes the largest share, simply because that situation is by far the most  
common.  
With P(a) in hand, the marginals of the leaf nodes follow in one line each:  
        P(j) = P(a) · P(j \| a) + P(¬a) · P(j \| ¬a) = 0.00252 × 0.90 + 0.99748 × 0.05 ≈ 0.0521  
        P(m) = P(a) · P(m \| a) + P(¬a) · P(m \| ¬a) = 0.00252 × 0.70 + 0.99748 × 0.01 ≈ 0.0117  
  
Query 3: A predictive (causal) query  
Question. Suppose a burglary is in progress. How likely is it that Mary calls?  
Here we reason forward along the arrows, from a cause to one of its effects. Earthquake and Alarm are  
not observed, so both must be summed out. Do it in two stages.  
Stage 1: probability of the alarm given a burglary. Because B and E are independent, P(e \| b) is just P(e).  
        P(a \| b) = P(e) · P(a \| b, e) + P(¬e) · P(a \| b, ¬e)  
        = 0.002 × 0.95 + 0.998 × 0.94 = 0.0019 + 0.9381 = 0.9400  
Stage 2: push the result through to Mary. Once A is known, M is independent of B, so P(m \| a, b) is  
simply P(m \| a).  
        P(m \| b) = P(a \| b) · P(m \| a) + P(¬a \| b) · P(m \| ¬a)  
        = 0.9400 × 0.70 + 0.0600 × 0.01 = 0.6580 + 0.0006 ≈ 0.659  
Interpretation. Even though the alarm is very reliable (94%), Mary only calls about two thirds of the  
time during a burglary, because she often misses the alarm. Compare this with the unconditional value  
P(m) ≈ 0.0117 from Query 2: knowing about the burglary raises the chance of Mary calling more than  
fifty fold.

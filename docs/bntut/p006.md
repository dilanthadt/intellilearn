5.4 One model, every direction of reasoning  
The same ten numbers answered a causal query (Query 3, from burglary to Mary), a diagnostic query  
(Query 4, from the calls back to burglary) and an intercausal query (Query 5, between burglary and  
earthquake). A classifier trained to predict burglary from calls could do only one of these. The network  
is a model of the domain, not of one particular question.  
  
5.5 Independence you can see  
The graph makes the independence assumptions explicit and checkable. Is it reasonable that John and  
Mary do not influence each other except through the alarm? Is it reasonable that burglaries and  
earthquakes are independent? These are modelling decisions you can discuss with a domain expert by  
looking at the picture, before any numbers are involved.  
  
 A subtle point worth testing yourself on  
 J and M are conditionally independent given A, but they are not independent outright. Check it:  
 P(m) ≈ 0.0117, but P(m \| j) = P(j, m) / P(j) = 0.002084 / 0.0521 ≈ 0.040. John calling more than  
 triples the chance that Mary calls, because it makes the alarm more likely. The graph tells you  
 this: J and M are connected through A, and the path is only blocked once A is observed.  
  
  
6. Exercises  
Work these by hand before looking at the answers. All of them reuse quantities computed above.  
    1. Compute P(a \| j), the probability that the alarm actually rang given that John called. Is John a  
       trustworthy witness on his own?  
    2. Compute P(a \| m). Compare it with your answer to Exercise 1 and explain, using the CPTs, why  
       Mary's call is stronger evidence than John's.  
    3. Compute P(b \| a), the probability of a burglary given that the alarm rang (but nobody has called  
       yet).  
    4. Compute P(b \| m) using the two stage method of Query 3 together with normalisation.  
    5. Verify the claim in Query 4 that P(e \| j, m) ≈ 0.176.  
    6. Suppose a new neighbour, Sam, is added who also listens for the alarm. Which CPTs change,  
       how many new numbers are needed, and how many entries would the full joint table have?  
  
  
7. Answers  
    1. P(a, j) = 0.00252 × 0.90 ≈ 0.002265 and P(j) ≈ 0.0521, so P(a \| j) ≈ 0.043. A call from John alone is  
       weak evidence; over 95% of his calls are false alarms, because his 5% error rate applied to the  
       vast number of quiet nights swamps the rare true alarms.  
    2. P(a, m) = 0.00252 × 0.70 ≈ 0.001762 and P(m) ≈ 0.0117, so P(a \| m) ≈ 0.150. Mary is about three  
       times more convincing. The reason is not her 0.70 hit rate (it is lower than John's 0.90) but her  
       0.01 false positive rate, five times lower than John's 0.05. What matters for evidential strength  
       is the ratio P(m \| a) / P(m \| ¬a) = 70 against P(j \| a) / P(j \| ¬a) = 18.

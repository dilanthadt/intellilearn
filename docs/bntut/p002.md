3. The one rule you need: the chain rule  
A Bayesian network encodes the full joint distribution as a product of local factors. Each node  
contributes one factor: its probability given its parents.  
        P(B, E, A, J, M) = P(B) · P(E) · P(A \| B, E) · P(J \| A) · P(M \| A)  
Compare this with the general chain rule of probability, which would write P(J \| A, B, E) and P(M \| J, A,  
B, E). The network is allowed to drop the extra variables because of the conditional independence  
assumptions built into its structure: once you know whether the alarm rang, knowing about the  
burglary tells you nothing further about whether John calls, and knowing that John called tells you  
nothing further about Mary.  
  
 Two other facts you will use repeatedly  
 Marginalisation (summing out): P(X) = Σy P(X, y). To remove a variable you do not care about, add  
 up over all its values.  
 Normalisation: P(X \| e) = α · P(X, e), where α = 1 / Σx P(x, e). Compute the unnormalised joint for  
 each value of X, then divide by their sum.  
  
  
4. Worked queries  
Query 1: A full joint probability  
Question. What is the probability that the alarm sounds, both John and Mary call, and there is neither a  
burglary nor an earthquake?  
This is the simplest kind of query because every variable is assigned a value. Just apply the chain rule  
and read the numbers from the tables.  
        P(¬b, ¬e, a, j, m) = P(¬b) · P(¬e) · P(a \| ¬b, ¬e) · P(j \| a) · P(m \| a)  
        = 0.999 × 0.998 × 0.001 × 0.90 × 0.70
